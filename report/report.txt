\title{SMS Spam Filtering with Naive Bayes, Linear SVM, and KNN:\\
A Reproduction of Almeida et al.\ (2011)}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Zachary Shrader (zjs5329)}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We reproduce the SMS spam filtering study of Almeida et al.\ using a from-scratch
multinomial Naive Bayes classifier, a linear SVM, and KNN on TF-IDF features, and we
add an exploratory k-means analysis and an SMS$\leftrightarrow$Enron cross-corpus test.
Our Naive Bayes is the only classifier that keeps blocked ham under our 5\% cost
threshold. All three classifiers are weaker than the paper's best results, and
cross-corpus transfer is poor in both directions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and Dataset}
The task is binary classification of SMS messages as spam (label 1) or ham (label 0).
Dataset: SMS Span Collection, https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/228/sms+spam+collection, Version: Original, retreval: 11/1/2026. 
Class balance: 5574 messages, 13.4% spam. The ``everything is ham'' baseline accuracy is
$1 - $ spam rate, i.e.\ 86.6%\%. Exact duplicate messages were removed keeping the first occurrence so that identical text
cannot appear in both training and test sets. 
Effect on results: This would mean that the model does not overtrain on specific spam messages.

\section{Text Processing}
Tokenization and lowercasing are handled by scikit-learn's vectorizers. FThe vocabulary (and IDF weights, when TF-IDF is
used) is fitted on training text only and then applied to the test text, which avoids
leaking test information into the features.

\section{Naive Bayes (Implemented from Scratch)}
We implement multinomial Naive Bayes with add-$\alpha$ smoothing. The class prior is
$\log P(c)=\log(n_c/n)$, and the feature log-probabilities are
\begin{equation}
\log\theta_{c,j}=\log\frac{N_{c,j}+\alpha}{\sum_{k}(N_{c,k}+\alpha)},
\end{equation}
where $N_{c,j}$ is the total feature mass of feature $j$ in class $c$. Posteriors are
computed in log space,
\begin{equation}
\log P(c\mid x)=\log P(x\mid c)+\log P(c)-\operatorname{logsumexp}_{c'}\big(\log P(x\mid c')+\log P(c')\big),
\end{equation}
so long messages do not underflow. With $\alpha=0$, any word unseen in a class has
$\log\theta=-\infty$ (and an empty class gives $0/0$); our implementation reports
these cases with a warning instead of hiding them.

\textbf{Agreement with scikit-learn.} The reference check in
\texttt{results/metrics.json} gives prediction agreement of $1.0$ and a maximum absolute
difference in log-probabilities of $2.84\times10^{-14}$, so our implementation matches
the reference to floating-point precision.

\textbf{Choice of $\alpha$.} We use $\alpha=0.5$. In the alpha sweep, smaller values
blocked far too much ham (blocked ham rose by 18\% going from $0.5$ to $0.1$), while
larger values were too inaccurate to be useful (fewer than 50\% of spam messages caught).

\section{Other Classifiers}
\textbf{Linear SVM.} $C$ was chosen using training data only, balancing F1 improvement
against the share of ham blocked: we doubled $C$ until blocked ham increased and then
explored the range around that point.

\textbf{KNN.} We used $k\in\{1,3,5\}$ with cosine distance, as in the paper.

\textbf{k-means exploration.} We clustered 50-dimensional LSI features with $k=2$. The
clusters did not recover the spam/ham split: homogeneity was 0.013, completeness 0.009,
V-measure 0.011, and adjusted Rand index $-0.043$ (chance level). The spam fraction was
8.5\% in cluster 0 and 16.2\% in cluster 1, both close to the base rate. This indicates
that spam versus ham is not the dominant geometric structure in these features, in
contrast to the supervised classifiers.

\textbf{Operating point.} A blocked legitimate message costs far more than a missed
spam message, because the user may never see the blocked message, whereas spam is a
nuisance. We treat more than 5\% blocked ham as unacceptable. Only Naive Bayes (3.13\%)
meets this bar. The KNN settings come from the paper, and the linear SVM showed no
meaningful decrease in blocked ham beyond a certain $C$.

\section{Comparison with Almeida et al.}
Our Naive Bayes is our strongest classifier but is weak compared to the paper's best
results. It is stronger than the paper's lower-performing Naive Bayes implementations
(higher SC, with blocked ham about the same). Our linear SVM is much weaker than the
paper's: blocking 41\% of ham is a very poor result. Our KNN is also much weaker, even
with the same $k$ values.

\section{Exploratory Task: Enron-Spam Transfer}
We used Enron~1 and trained on one corpus and tested on the other. Spam rates differ
(SMS 12.6\%, Enron 29.3\%), and vocabulary and style differ more. Results:

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Cross-corpus transfer.}
\label{tab:cross}
\begin{tabular}{llcccc}
\toprule
Direction & Model & Acc. & F1 & MCC & ROC-AUC \\
\midrule
SMS$\to$Enron & NB  & 0.569 & 0.163 & $-0.122$ & 0.434 \\
SMS$\to$Enron & SVM & 0.633 & 0.376 & 0.116 & 0.560 \\
Enron$\to$SMS & NB  & 0.817 & 0.302 & 0.197 & 0.664 \\
Enron$\to$SMS & SVM & 0.278 & 0.234 & 0.054 & 0.560 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Transfer was weak in both directions. SMS$\to$Enron Naive Bayes was worse than random
(AUC 0.43, MCC $-0.12$). Enron$\to$SMS Naive Bayes was modestly above chance in ranking
(AUC 0.66) but still below the all-ham accuracy of 87.4\%. The Enron$\to$SMS SVM flagged
about 81\% of ham as spam, a threshold-calibration failure rather than only a ranking
failure. These are single runs without confidence intervals, so the NB-versus-SVM
differences are suggestive, not conclusive.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
Our from-scratch Naive Bayes matches scikit-learn and is the only classifier that
satisfies our blocked-ham constraint, but all classifiers trail the paper's best
results, and none transfers well across corpora. Limitations: single runs, no
confidence intervals.
